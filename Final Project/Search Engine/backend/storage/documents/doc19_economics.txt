Economics studies how individuals, firms, and governments allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited wants and needs. It is divided into microeconomics, which examines individual actors and markets, and macroeconomics, which studies aggregate phenomena like total output, unemployment, and price levels.

Supply and demand is the foundational model of markets. The law of demand states that quantity demanded falls as price rises, all else equal. The law of supply states that quantity supplied rises with price. Market equilibrium is reached where the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded. Shifts in demand or supply curves, caused by income changes, technology advances, or preference shifts, move the equilibrium price and quantity.

Elasticity measures the responsiveness of one variable to changes in another. Price elasticity of demand quantifies how much quantity demanded changes with a one percent change in price. Goods with close substitutes tend to be more elastic. Total revenue maximization depends on whether demand is elastic or inelastic at the current price level.

Market structures range from perfect competition through monopolistic competition and oligopoly to monopoly. Perfect competition features many buyers and sellers, homogeneous products, and free entry and exit, leading to zero long-run economic profit. Monopolists face no close competition and can set price above marginal cost, earning positive economic profit while causing deadweight loss through reduced output.

Game theory studies strategic interactions where the outcome for each participant depends on the choices of others. The Nash equilibrium is a set of strategies where no player can improve their payoff by unilaterally changing their strategy. The prisoner's dilemma illustrates how individually rational choices can lead to collectively suboptimal outcomes, motivating cooperation mechanisms and regulation.

Macroeconomics analyzes the total output of an economy measured by gross domestic product. GDP can be calculated as the sum of consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports. Business cycles describe the recurring expansion and contraction of economic activity. Recessions are defined as two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth and are associated with rising unemployment.

Monetary policy is conducted by central banks through adjustments to short-term interest rates and the money supply. Lowering interest rates stimulates borrowing and investment during recessions. Raising rates curbs inflationary pressure when the economy overheats. Quantitative easing involves central banks purchasing government bonds to inject liquidity when conventional interest rate tools reach their lower bound.

Fiscal policy involves government spending and taxation decisions. Expansionary fiscal policy increases spending or cuts taxes to stimulate demand during recessions. Contractionary policy raises taxes or cuts spending to cool an overheated economy. Automatic stabilizers like unemployment insurance and progressive income taxes naturally cushion economic fluctuations without legislative action.

International trade allows countries to specialize in goods and services they produce most efficiently. Comparative advantage shows that trade is mutually beneficial even when one country is more productive at everything. Tariffs and quotas protect domestic industries from foreign competition but raise prices for consumers. Trade agreements reduce barriers and coordinate regulations across multiple countries.

Behavioral economics integrates psychological insights into economic models. Traditional economics assumes rational maximizing agents, but empirical evidence shows that people systematically deviate from rationality. Loss aversion means losses feel more painful than equivalent gains feel rewarding. Choice architecture and nudge theory design environments to guide people toward better decisions without restricting freedom.
