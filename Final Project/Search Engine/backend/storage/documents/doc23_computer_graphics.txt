Computer graphics is the field that studies methods for digitally synthesizing, manipulating, and rendering visual content. From video games and animated films to medical imaging and scientific visualization, computer graphics transforms mathematical descriptions into images perceived by human eyes.

The graphics pipeline is the sequence of stages that transforms three-dimensional scene geometry into a two-dimensional image on a display. The application stage runs on the CPU and produces a stream of geometric primitives. The vertex processing stage applies transformation matrices to convert vertices from object space through world space to clip space. Rasterization converts triangles into discrete pixels. The fragment processing stage computes the color of each pixel using lighting models and texture samples.

Coordinate transformations move geometry between different reference frames. Model matrices transform objects from local object space to world space. The view matrix transforms world space into camera space, placing the camera at the origin looking along a canonical direction. The projection matrix maps camera space to clip space, encoding perspective foreshortening so that distant objects appear smaller.

Rasterization converts vector geometry into a pixel grid representation called a raster. Scan conversion determines which pixels fall inside each triangle using edge equations or barycentric coordinates. Depth buffering resolves visibility by keeping the fragment with the smallest depth value at each pixel. Anti-aliasing reduces jagged edges by sampling multiple subpixel locations and averaging colors.

Shading models simulate how light interacts with surfaces. The Phong model combines ambient, diffuse, and specular components. Ambient light provides a constant base illumination. Diffuse reflection scatters light equally in all directions proportional to the cosine of the angle between the light direction and surface normal. Specular reflection produces highlights that shift with viewing angle, simulating glossy surfaces.

Textures map two-dimensional images onto three-dimensional surfaces to add fine detail without additional geometry. UV coordinates assign each vertex a position in texture image space. Mipmapping precomputes downsampled versions of textures to reduce aliasing when surfaces are viewed at a distance. Bump and normal mapping perturb surface normals from stored data to simulate fine-scale surface detail at low geometric cost.

Ray tracing models light transport by casting rays from the camera through each pixel into the scene and tracing their interactions with surfaces. Primary rays find the first surface intersection. Shadow rays test visibility to light sources. Secondary rays handle reflections, refractions, and global illumination effects. Monte Carlo path tracing simulates indirect lighting by recursively sampling random ray directions, converging to physically accurate images at the cost of high sample counts.

Graphics processing units are massively parallel processors designed to accelerate graphics workloads. Thousands of shader cores execute vertex and fragment programs simultaneously on independent primitives and fragments. Modern GPUs also support general-purpose computing through compute shaders and frameworks like CUDA and OpenCL, enabling scientific simulation, machine learning inference, and image processing.

Physically based rendering simulates light according to the principles of physics, improving realism and artistic consistency. The bidirectional reflectance distribution function describes how a surface reflects light from any incoming direction to any outgoing direction. Energy conservation ensures that surfaces cannot reflect more light than they receive. Metalness and roughness parameters provide an intuitive material model compatible with global illumination.

Real-time rendering balances visual quality against the constraint of producing frames in milliseconds for interactive applications. Level of detail systems swap high-polygon models for simpler ones as objects recede. Culling algorithms skip rendering objects outside the viewing frustum or occluded by other geometry. Deferred shading decouples geometry processing from lighting, enabling many light sources without proportional performance cost.
