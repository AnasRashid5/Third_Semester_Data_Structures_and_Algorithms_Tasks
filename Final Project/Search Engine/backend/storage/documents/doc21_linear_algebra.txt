Linear algebra is the branch of mathematics that studies vectors, vector spaces, linear transformations, and systems of linear equations. It underpins nearly every area of modern science and engineering, from machine learning and computer graphics to quantum mechanics and structural analysis.

Vectors are mathematical objects with both magnitude and direction. In coordinate geometry, a vector in two or three dimensions is an ordered list of numbers called components. Vector addition combines two vectors component-by-component. Scalar multiplication scales all components by a number. The dot product of two vectors equals the sum of products of corresponding components and encodes the angle between them.

Vector spaces are sets of vectors closed under addition and scalar multiplication and satisfying eight axioms. The real number line, the plane, three-dimensional space, and polynomial rings are all vector spaces. A basis is a minimal set of vectors that spans the entire space through linear combinations. The dimension of a vector space is the number of vectors in any basis.

Matrices are rectangular arrays of numbers used to represent linear transformations between vector spaces. Matrix addition adds corresponding entries. Matrix multiplication composes two transformations: the entry in row i and column j of the product equals the dot product of row i of the first matrix with column j of the second. Matrix multiplication is associative but not generally commutative.

The determinant of a square matrix encodes how a transformation scales volumes. A determinant of zero indicates that the transformation collapses the space to a lower dimension, meaning the matrix is singular and has no inverse. The determinant can be computed by cofactor expansion along any row or column. Cramer's rule expresses the solution to a linear system using ratios of determinants.

Gaussian elimination transforms a system of linear equations into row echelon form through row operations. Back substitution then solves for each variable in turn from the last equation upward. The reduced row echelon form produced by Gauss-Jordan elimination directly reveals the solution. Pivoting strategies improve numerical stability when divisions by small numbers would amplify rounding errors.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors describe the intrinsic behavior of a linear transformation. An eigenvector is a nonzero vector that is scaled rather than rotated by the transformation. The corresponding eigenvalue is the scaling factor. Eigenvalues are roots of the characteristic polynomial obtained by setting the determinant of the matrix minus a scalar multiple of the identity to zero.

Matrix decompositions factor matrices into products that reveal structure and simplify computation. The LU decomposition splits a matrix into lower and upper triangular factors for efficient system solving. The QR decomposition factors a matrix into an orthogonal matrix and an upper triangular matrix, used in least squares and eigenvalue algorithms. Singular value decomposition is the most general decomposition, underpinning principal component analysis.

Orthogonality generalizes perpendicularity to arbitrary vector spaces. Two vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero. An orthonormal basis consists of mutually orthogonal unit vectors. The Gram-Schmidt process transforms any basis into an orthonormal basis. Orthogonal projections find the closest point in a subspace to a given vector, the foundation of least squares regression.

Applications of linear algebra permeate modern computing. Neural network layers apply matrix multiplications to transform activation vectors. Graphics processors use matrix operations to project three-dimensional scenes onto two-dimensional screens. Search engines represent documents as vectors and compute cosine similarity to rank relevance. Quantum computing represents quantum states as complex vectors in high-dimensional Hilbert spaces.
