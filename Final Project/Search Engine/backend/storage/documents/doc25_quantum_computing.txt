Quantum computing harnesses the principles of quantum mechanics to perform computations that are intractable for classical computers. While a classical bit holds either zero or one, a quantum bit, or qubit, can exist in a superposition of both states simultaneously, enabling quantum computers to explore many solution paths at once.

Quantum mechanics provides the physical laws governing qubits. Superposition allows a qubit to be in any combination of zero and one states until measured, at which point it collapses to a definite value with a probability determined by the amplitudes of its state vector. Entanglement correlates the states of multiple qubits such that measuring one instantly determines information about the others, even at a distance.

Quantum gates manipulate qubits analogously to how classical logic gates manipulate bits. The Hadamard gate places a qubit in an equal superposition of zero and one. The Pauli-X gate flips a qubit from zero to one or vice versa. The CNOT gate flips a target qubit conditioned on the state of a control qubit, enabling entanglement. Quantum circuits compose these gates into algorithms.

Quantum parallelism evaluates a function on all possible inputs simultaneously through superposition. However, measuring the quantum state collapses it to a single outcome, so raw parallelism cannot directly extract all results. Quantum algorithms exploit interference to amplify probabilities of correct answers while suppressing wrong ones, achieving speedups for specific problem classes.

Shor's algorithm factors large integers exponentially faster than the best classical algorithms. Classical integer factoring underlies the security of RSA public-key cryptography, so large-scale quantum computers could break widely deployed encryption schemes. This motivates research into post-quantum cryptography algorithms based on mathematical problems believed to be hard for quantum computers.

Grover's algorithm searches an unsorted database of N items in time proportional to the square root of N, achieving a quadratic speedup over classical linear search. While less dramatic than the exponential speedups of some algorithms, Grover's result has broad applicability wherever search underlies an optimization or verification task.

Quantum error correction combats decoherence, the tendency of qubits to lose their quantum state due to interactions with the environment. Physical qubits are too noisy to directly implement long computations. Error correction encodes one logical qubit into many physical qubits and uses redundancy to detect and correct errors without measuring the quantum state directly. Surface codes are among the most promising near-term error correction codes.

Quantum hardware platforms differ in how physical qubits are implemented. Superconducting circuits cooled to near absolute zero produce qubits from Josephson junctions. Trapped ion systems confine individual ions in electromagnetic traps and manipulate them with lasers. Photonic systems encode qubits in polarization or time-bin degrees of freedom of photons. Each platform offers different trade-offs in coherence time, gate fidelity, and scalability.

Near-term quantum devices, called noisy intermediate-scale quantum devices, contain tens to hundreds of physical qubits without error correction. Variational quantum algorithms use classical optimization loops to tune parameterized quantum circuits, tolerating noise better than fully coherent algorithms. Quantum machine learning investigates whether quantum devices can accelerate specific learning tasks.

Quantum supremacy experiments demonstrated that quantum computers can perform specific well-defined tasks faster than any classical computer. These experiments used random circuit sampling, generating outputs that are computationally difficult to verify classically. While not yet applicable to practical problems, these demonstrations validated the underlying principles of quantum computational advantage.
